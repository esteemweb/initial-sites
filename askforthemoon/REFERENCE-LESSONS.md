# Reference lessons: moneyincheck.org

What the *Money in Check* site (a novel by Óscar Pérez) worked out about selling one book with a
website, what it got wrong, and what we take from it. I studied it at desktop and phone width,
read all the copy, and tried every control: the spread carousel, the language picker, the format
table, both buy buttons, the footer links. The chapter-one form was looked at, not submitted.

---

## The argument

**The case in three sentences.** Money runs on rules nobody taught you, and working hard doesn't
exempt you from them. This book teaches those rules through a story about a scholarship boy at
Harvard, so you learn them the way he does, by losing first. The author has spent his career in
rooms with people who understand these rules, and he is passing on what he saw.

**The objection it answers.** "I don't want a finance book, and I don't believe a novel can teach
me anything." This is a genre objection, and the site answers it head-on in its best piece of copy:
*Not a finance textbook. Not just a novel.* It admits the book is two things, and claims that being
two things is the point rather than a weakness. A second, quieter objection is price, and it
handles that by showing the cheapest format in the header and noting that the book is free on
Kindle Unlimited.

**How it establishes the author.** Through a CV: a list of cities, three famous company names, a
founder title, a signature. The bio is surrounded by small captioned images from his career, such
as an award object, a conference marquee and books he co-wrote. Each is dated, which reads as
evidence rather than adjectives. But the evidence is from the wrong field. It proves he ran a
successful design-awards company, not that he understands wealth. The bridge between the two is
one asserted sentence ("a firsthand understanding of how those who truly grasp the rules of
money… think"), and nothing on the page backs it up.

**How it proves the book is good without letting you read it.** Mostly by showing its interior.
The carousel shows open spreads taken from the middle and late in the book, not the opening.
They're typeset properly, legible on desktop, and include a chart of interest rates and a
hand-annotated diagram of a four-part cycle. So you can see what kind of book this is: prose that
stops for data, a narrator who says "So I did it." There are no reviews and no quotes. The only
full sample, the first chapter, sits behind an email address.

## The sequence

**The order it reveals things in:**
1. The title as an object, and a dateline.
2. The thesis: one long sentence addressed to "most people" that names the reader's problem.
3. The book: the object, then formats and price, then the synopsis.
4. A chapter-one sample in exchange for an email.
5. The author.
6. A closing aphorism.

The logic is the self-help sequence: problem, then solution, then salesman. It works for a book
sold as useful. It is the wrong order for a book whose value depends on who wrote it.

**What it leads with.** The problem, dressed as the title. The first sentence you actually read is
about *you*, not the book or the author. The book isn't described until you've accepted the
premise.

**Where it first asks for money.** Immediately. The header carries a "Buy it now!" link with the
cheapest price beside it, visible before a word of the argument, and it goes straight to Amazon.
The real ask comes in the book section, next to the synopsis. By then it has earned the premise
but not the author. On the phone layout the price table appears before the synopsis, so you're
told the cost before you're told what happens.

**What it holds back.** Almost all of the plot: only the premise and the protagonist's situation
are given. It also holds back the first chapter (gated), the author's face (small, late) and any
outside praise. Holding back plot is right; it makes the spreads do the work. Holding back the
author is a mistake for this kind of book. Gating the sample puts friction at exactly the moment
of highest interest.

## The craft

**How it feels like a book rather than a product page.**
- The whole page sits on a faint ruled grid, like a ledger or graph paper.
- A real chess game is written in pencil-like handwriting in the margins. The moves appear one by
  one as you scroll, like a scoresheet being kept while you read.
- The nav carries an edition statement, as a title page would.
- Under the title, a dateline gives your current date and time, updating live, next to the city
  where the novel is set. It is the way a chapter or a letter opens: the story is happening now.
- The thesis is revealed word by word as you scroll, which forces reading pace on one sentence.
- The interior spreads show paper texture, running heads and folios.

**What it does that a generic landing page wouldn't.** It trusts a single sentence to carry a whole
screen. It shows inside pages instead of a list of benefits. It gives the book's central metaphor,
chess, to the page itself rather than explaining it in copy. The scoresheet never announces itself.
A landing page would have had "What you'll learn" bullets. This one refuses, because saying the
lessons out loud would turn the novel back into the textbook it claims not to be.

**Where the restraint is.** No reviews, no star ratings, no press logos, no bullet list of lessons,
no countdown, no bonus stack. The site is a single page with no subpages. The synopsis stays short.
The author photo is small.

**The single best idea.** The chess game in the margins. It is a legitimate game notation that plays
out as you move down the page, never explained or labelled, visible only if you know how to read
it. It makes the book's claim, that there are invisible rules in plain sight you haven't learned to
read, into something you experience on the page rather than something you're told. Readers who
can read chess notation feel let in; everyone else feels the texture without knowing why. It
repeats the book's premise without spelling it out. A close second is taking the sample spreads
from deep inside the book rather than the opening. Readers already know what a first page looks
like. A page from the middle shows what the book actually is.

## The mechanics

**Sampling.** There are two tiers. First, legible interior spreads in a carousel, which you can
read on desktop but which shrink to unreadable single pages on a phone. Second, the first chapter
to read and to listen to, sent in exchange for an email. The spreads give away texture and voice
but no story. The chapter is the real sample, and it is gated.

**Social proof.** None about the book. The only proof on the page is the author's CV artefacts.
Those are convincing that he exists and succeeded, and decorative on the question of whether the
book is good. Leaving out invented or thin praise is the right call. It means the book has to prove
itself, and it only half does.

**Buying.** A single retailer, and a single link. The format table lists paperback, hardcover,
Kindle and an audiobook marked "soon". It looks like a choice, but the rows aren't clickable, and
the button and the header link both go to the same US Amazon paperback page whatever format you
wanted. Prices are in dollars only, on a site offered in five languages. The listing Amazon shows
also sells the book under a self-help subtitle, which undercuts the site's "not just a novel"
framing.

**The fact that you can't hold it.** Three answers, one weak and two strong:
- The weak one is a stock-style photo of a hand holding the book in front of a bookshelf. It is
  the product-listing move the rest of the site avoids.
- The spreads are a real answer: paper grain, folios, type set as it is in print.
- Offering the chapter as audio is another way of possessing the book before owning it.

## What it gets wrong

- **The credential doesn't match the subject.** It proves success in one field and asks you to
  transfer that trust to another. The one sentence bridging the gap is claimed, not shown.
- **The author comes after the ask.** For a book that depends on who wrote it, this is backwards.
- **The recurring motifs are too literal.** Floating dollar bills and a coin drift across almost
  every section. The first one is a flourish; by the fifth it is noise, and it cheapens the far
  cleverer scoresheet in the margins.
- **The hand-and-bookshelf mockup** sits right next to the careful spreads, and loses to them.
- **The buy flow is fake-choice.** Four formats, one destination, one currency, one territory. The
  "Buy it now!" exclamation mark reads like a sales pitch.
- **The synopsis is about themes, not events:** "wealth, ambition, family, and freedom." That
  describes any book. The first paragraph (the scholarship, the oversized suitcase) is specific and
  good; the rest retreats into abstraction.
- **The closing line is a generic aphorism** that could end any productivity book. The site ends
  weaker than it starts.
- **Phone layout:** the spreads become unreadable, the price moves above the synopsis, and the
  word-by-word reveal is slow when all you can do is scroll.

**What I'd cut:** the currency, all but one of the flying banknotes, the bookshelf mockup, the
exclamation mark, the closing aphorism, the city list in the bio, and the email gate on chapter
one.

**Where it loses me:** the author section. Until then the site has been clever and confident.
Then it reads a CV aloud (cities, brand names, a signature) to a reader who wanted one reason to
believe *this person* knows *this subject*, and doesn't get it.

## Transferable lessons

1. **Establish the author before describing the book, whenever the author is the reason to
   believe it.** A stranger's book about a subject is a claim; a practitioner's is testimony.
   Order the page so the reader meets the witness before the evidence.

2. **Credibility must be about the book's subject, and shown rather than asserted.** One specific,
   verifiable fact from the field beats a list of prestigious names from outside it. If you need a
   sentence to bridge the author's experience and the book's subject, the gap is already visible.

3. **Name the reader's genre objection in the site's own voice, early and in one line.** Every
   crossover book triggers "is this a novel or a manual?" Say what it is and what it isn't before
   the reader decides for you.

4. **Let the book's central idea shape how the page behaves, and never label it.** The strongest
   move is one the reader experiences before they can explain it. If it needs a caption, it's
   decoration. Use it once, as a layer that runs through the page, not as a repeated ornament.

5. **Sample from inside the book, not just the opening, and make every sample readable at every
   size.** A page from the middle proves the book holds up. A page you can't read at phone width
   is a picture of a book, not a sample.

6. **Don't charge for the sample.** An email gate is a price, charged at the moment of highest
   interest. Give the reading freely. If you want an address, ask for it after the sample, for
   something more.

7. **Don't show a price before giving a reason.** A quiet, persistent way to buy is courtesy. A
   price in the reader's face before they know what the book is makes it a product listing. The
   first real ask comes after the book has been sampled and the author believed.

8. **Write the synopsis as what happens to someone, not what the book is about.** Themes are shared
   by every book on the shelf. A named person, a specific situation and a concrete detail belong
   only to this one.

9. **Make buying honest.** If formats are listed, choosing one must lead somewhere different. Show
   the reader's currency and the retailers that serve their country. Offer a way to buy that isn't
   the biggest retailer, because a site that sends everyone to one store is an advert for that store.

10. **Make the book tangible using things only books have**: pages, running heads, folios, an
    edition statement, a colophon, the author's voice aloud. A hand holding a mockup in front of a
    bookshelf is the product-listing answer; avoid it.

11. **Show no social proof rather than decorative social proof.** Praise that isn't specific,
    attributed and earned costs more trust than it buys. If the book has no reviews yet, the
    sample and the author carry the weight, and the page should be built so they can.

12. **End on the book's own line, not a borrowed aphorism.** The last thing the reader sees before
    the final ask should be something only this book could say.

13. **Decide the phone sequence separately; don't just stack the desktop layout.** When the layout
    collapses to one column, the order of the argument changes. Check that the phone reader still
    meets the author, then the book, then the price, in that order.
